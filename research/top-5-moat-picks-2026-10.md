## 3. Moat studies

### #1 S&P Global (SPGI): score 89
**What it sells:** credit ratings, the S&P 500 and other indices, Platts commodity price benchmarks, and Market Intelligence data. The Mobility (CARFAX) business was spun off on Jul 1, 2026.

**Moat sources:**
- **Regulatory and reputational barrier (ratings).** Bond issuers need ratings from recognized agencies to reach investors, and many investment mandates and regulations reference those ratings. S&P and Moody's together hold roughly 80% of the global market. No new entrant has broken in for decades. Issuers pay because investors demand the rating, and investors trust it because it is S&P.
- **Network effect and switching costs (indices).** The S&P 500 is *the* benchmark. Trillions of dollars in index funds and ETFs pay fees linked to assets tracking it, and every active manager is measured against it. Swapping the benchmark would break decades of performance history.
- **Benchmark pricing (Platts).** Oil, LNG and metals contracts are written against Platts assessments, which embeds them in physical trading.

**Evidence (Q2 2026, pro forma ex-Mobility):**
- Revenue +11% to $3.68B. Adjusted operating margin 54.3%.
- **Ratings** revenue +17% at a 68.5% margin. **Indices** revenue +20% at a 71.5% margin.
- EPS +26%.
- Benchmark products are ~2/3 of revenue and over 80% of operating profit.
- It has raised its dividend for 50+ consecutive years.

**Why it's cheap:** fear that AI agents will commoditize financial data. That fear applies to Market Intelligence (36% margin, +6% growth), *not* to ratings or indices, which make most of the profit. At ~20x 2026 EPS it trades at its lowest multiple since 2022, against a usual 25–30x.

**What would break the thesis:** regulators removing the role of ratings or banning issuer-pays (discussed after 2008, never done); major fee compression on index licensing; a reputational scandal of 2008 scale.

**Guru check:** Ackman (new, Q2), Chris Hohn (TCI, 10.9%), Dev Kantesaria (Valley Forge, 19.9%).

### #2 Visa (V): score 83
**What it sells:** a toll on each card payment over its network. Banks issue the cards and carry the credit risk; Visa only moves the messages and settles.

**Moat sources:**
- **Two-sided network effect.** Billions of cards and well over 100M merchant locations. Merchants accept Visa because consumers carry it, and consumers carry it because everyone accepts it. Very few networks have reached this scale.
- **Cost advantage and scale.** The fixed-cost network carries enormous volume, producing net margins above 50% with almost no capital needed.
- **Brand and trust, plus fraud data.** Decades of transaction data feed fraud tools that smaller rails cannot match.

**Evidence (Q3 FY26):** net revenue +14% to $11.6B, EPS +11%. Payments volume passed **$4 trillion in a single quarter**; processed transactions +10%; cross-border +12%.

**Why it's in the list despite a smaller discount:** it is the widest moat in this screen. At ~24x FY27 EPS it is below its 5-year median (~26x). Munger's rule applies: a wonderful business at a fair price beats a fair business at a wonderful price.

**What would break the thesis (the risks are real):**
- The **Credit Card Competition Act**, reintroduced in Jan 2026 and endorsed by President Trump, would force alternative routing on credit cards.
- The **DOJ debit monopoly lawsuit** (filed Sep 2024) is still in progress.
- **Stablecoins and instant bank-to-bank payment rails** could bypass card fees. Visa is building stablecoin settlement into its own network to stay in the flow.

Because of these, its safety score is 8.5 rather than 10.

**Guru check:** Hohn (TCI, 19.8%), Ackman (new), Terry Smith. Owned by 30 of the ~82 superinvestors Dataroma tracks. Berkshire sold its small stake in Q1 while consolidating its portfolio.

### #3 Aon (AON): score 82
**What it sells:** insurance and reinsurance brokerage, risk consulting, and health and wealth advice for corporations. Aon earns commissions and fees but **takes no underwriting risk**.

**Moat sources:**
- **Efficient scale and oligopoly.** Large corporate risk is dominated by a handful of global brokers: Marsh, Aon, WTW and Gallagher. Placing a Fortune 500 company's complex program needs a global network, data and market clout a new entrant can't match.
- **Switching costs.** Client relationships last decades and renewals are sticky; a broker switch is risky and disruptive for a CFO.
- **Data and analytics.** Aon's proprietary loss and pricing data improves its placement and advice.

**Evidence:** recurring, asset-light, fee-based revenue that rises with insurance premiums and inflation. Investment-grade ratings (Baa2/A-) are expected to hold through the USI deal.

**Why it's cheap:** on Aug 31, 2026, Aon agreed to buy **USI for $17B in cash, funded entirely with debt**, and paused buybacks. The stock fell 9.5% that day and now sits at its 52-week low, ~29% below its high, at ~15x earnings versus a usual ~20–25x. The deal is priced at ~14.5x synergized EBITDA, with $395M of run-rate synergies. It dilutes EPS in 2027 and adds to EPS from 2028.

**What would break the thesis:** the integration fails, debt stays high without being paid down, organic growth stays below ~3%, or AI tools commoditize mid-market brokerage.

**Guru check:** owned by 10 superinvestors. Berkshire exited in Q1, as part of Abel's portfolio clean-up rather than a negative call on the business.

### #4 Berkshire Hathaway (BRK.B): score 82
**What it is:** insurance float (GEICO, General Re, Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance), BNSF railroad, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, ~$344B of stocks including the Japanese trading houses, dozens of manufacturing and retail businesses, and **$365.5B of cash**.

**Moat sources:**
- **Cost-free capital (float).** Insurance premiums are invested before claims are paid, at a cost of zero or less.
- **Hard-to-copy assets.** BNSF's rail network cannot be rebuilt. Utilities are regulated monopolies.
- **Reputation as "buyer of last resort".** In crises, companies come to Berkshire for capital on Berkshire's terms.
- **Diversification.** The holding company is itself diversified across rail, energy, insurance, manufacturing, retail and a stock portfolio.

**Evidence (Q2 2026):**
- Operating earnings $12.98B (+16%). Manufacturing, service and retail +24%; BNSF +6%; BHE +27%.
- Net buyer of stocks for the first time in 15 quarters, and ~$4.8B of buybacks so far in 2026.

**Why it fits even though it isn't cheap:** at ~1.43x book (Morningstar fair value $510) it's fairly priced, not a bargain. It is the **safety anchor**: the least likely of the five to lose money over 10 years, and its cash pile is a built-in hedge for a crash. It pays **no dividend**, which avoids dividend withholding tax for non-US investors.

**What would break the thesis:** capital allocation deteriorates after Buffett (overpaying for large acquisitions), or a catastrophic insurance loss. Abel's first year has been disciplined: it cut 16 small positions, bought Alphabet and Delta, and has kept cash.

**Guru check:** Li Lu (15%, added), Guy Spier (~50%), Tom Russo (12%). Owned by 26 superinvestors.

### #5 American Express (AXP): score 81
**What it sells:** premium charge and credit cards. It is a **closed loop**: issuer, network and merchant acquirer in one company.

**Moat sources:**
- **Closed-loop data and pricing power.** It sees both sides of each transaction, which supports higher merchant fees and better risk models.
- **Brand and affluent customer base.** These cardholders spend more and default less. Its net write-off rate is 2.0%, among the lowest in the industry.
- **Fee-based model.** Annual card fees (Platinum, Gold) are a large and growing revenue stream that resists recession better than interest income.
- **Coalition of travel and lifestyle partners.** Airlines, hotels and lounges make the premium value proposition hard to copy.

**Evidence (Q2 2026):** EPS $4.53 (+11%). Billed business $455.8B (+9%). Write-offs flat at 2.0%. Full-year revenue growth guide raised to ~10%; FY26 EPS guide $17.30–17.90.

**Why it's cheap:**
- Higher marketing and technology spending, fear of a cap on card interest rates, and stablecoin headlines took it 21% off its high, to 4.7% above its 52-week low.
- At ~17x 2026 EPS, it is below the ~25x it traded at in late 2025.

**What would break the thesis:** a deep recession pushing write-offs above ~3.5% for long, a binding rate cap, or younger customers moving away from premium cards.

**Guru check:** Berkshire's #2 position (17.1%, held ~30 years, Buffett said he'd never sell), Guy Spier (15.3%).

## 4. The honest caveat: all five are financial or financial-adjacent

Their profit drivers differ: debt issuance and index fees, payment volume, insurance premiums, a diversified conglomerate, and premium spending plus credit. But **a 2008-style financial crisis would hit them at the same time**. In 2007–09, AmEx fell more than 80% and Moody's about 75%.

Mitigations:
1. **Monthly buying** turns a crash into cheaper shares rather than a forced sale.
2. **Berkshire's** diversification and cash cushion the portfolio.
3. To diversify, **swap the AXP or AON slot for PepsiCo (score 78)**.

**Value-tilt rules:**
1. A stock in its **Strong buy** zone gets double its slot. Fund that by skipping whichever stock is furthest into **Pause**, or failing that the one with the lowest score.
2. A stock in **Pause** gets $0 that month. Its money goes to the cheapest stock relative to its Buy price.
3. Never let one stock exceed ~30% of the portfolio.
4. Re-score each holding after its quarterly earnings. **Sell only if the moat breaks** (see "What would break the thesis" above), never because of price drops alone.

**If you can only buy whole shares, or pay a fixed fee per trade:** buy two stocks a month in rotation.
- Month 1: SPGI + AXP
- Month 2: AON + V
- Month 3: BRK.B + SPGI

Repeat the cycle, choosing first whichever stock is in its strong-buy zone that month.

### If you are a non-US investor
